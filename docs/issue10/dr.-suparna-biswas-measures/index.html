The Risk Beyond the Cutoff: Limitations of VaR and the Need for Coherent Measures

Dr. Suparna Biswas
IISER Tirupati
Sep 05, 2026
This article discusses the fundamental principles of quantifying financial risk based on mathematical frameworks, the shortcomings of traditional value at risk (VaR) and clarifying how coherent risk measures address these issues. Four axioms of coherence are explored, describing how diversification reduces risk. A brief overview of the statistical estimation of VaR is given, explaining the use of empirical data to assess risk in situations where the underlying distribution remains unknown. An illustrative portfolio example which focuses VaR’s shortcomings in tackling the severity of tail risk, emphasizing the need for coherent risk measures. The article concludes by highlighting the fundamental research problem in the estimation of financial risk.

Reviewed by Shriparna Chattopadhyay

Each substantial financial choice entails a degree of uncertainty. A person preparing for retirement cannot foresee future market performance. A company that invests in emerging technologies cannot predict the project’s success. Financial institutions that provide loans face the risk of borrower default on repayments. In every case, uncertainty presents the possibility of adverse outcomes, which we denote as “risk”. In finance “risk” can be defined as the variability associated with unexpected changes in the values of assets and liabilities [5].

The challenge of managing risk is not exclusive to contemporary financial markets. Throughout history, individuals have looked for methods to safeguard themselves from unpredictable occurrences. Merchants of antiquity navigating extensive trade routes encountered threats such as theft, storms, and the peril of shipwrecks. To mitigate these risks, they established frameworks that enabled the distribution of losses among numerous participants [6]. Similar concepts gradually developed into the insurance contracts and financial institutions we have today.

As economies grew more intricate, the necessity for quantitative approaches to evaluate risk became ever more significant. Investors, banks, insurance companies, and regulators all need tools that address a crucial question: “What level of risk is being assumed?” Although risk is frequently talked about in casual terms, successful risk management necessitates a quantitative assessment that can evaluate various financial situations and ascertain the capital required to endure possible losses.

For numerous years, professionals depended on their intuition and experience to evaluate financial risk. Nonetheless, the swift expansion of financial markets throughout the twentieth century generated a need for more thorough and methodical strategies. This prompted researchers to explore the essential properties that a suitable measure of risk should possess.

Delbaen [4] and Artzner et al. ([1], [2]) made major contributions to the field of financial mathematics. Their work constitutes a notable contribution in the development of quantitative theory of financial mathematics. The authors define the “axioms of coherence” with extraordinary financial insight, which lead them to identify a minimal set of properties that a portfolio statistics must satisfy. Intrinsic concepts contained in the four “axioms of coherence” constitute the idea of risk.

The introduction of this framework significantly influenced financial mathematics. It was revealed that certain commonly utilized measures, such as VaR, have significant shortcomings despite their widespread acceptance in both industry and regulation. Grasping these strengths and weaknesses is a key goal of contemporary risk management. “Risk measure” and “measure of risk” are used interchangeably here.

VaR and its shortcomings

In 1997, these axioms revealed a major flaw in VaR, even though it has been widely used by the regulatory frameworks and financial organizations [1]. Because VaR does not adhere to the subadditivity assumption, it breaks the diversification principle. However, it satisfies the rest of the three axioms. Interpretation and Critique

Let’s try to understand Definition 2. A 5% VaR can be interpreted as the loss incurred in the best of the 5% worst-case scenarios, or equivalently, in the worst of the 95% best-case scenarios of the probability distribution of the future profit and loss. VaR is mistakenly seen as an accurate representation of prospective losses that may occur with a 5% likelihood. Two critical flaws are:

  • From Definition 2, we see that VaR consistently undervalues the risks of the worst-case scenarios it chooses, favoring the least dangerous one.
  • VaR exhibits a complete lack of sensitivity to the severity of the discarded worst-case scenarios, allowing for the possibility of encountering situations where two portfolios, despite having distinctly different risk profiles, can still present identical VaR values.

Research Problem

VaR remains the most widely used measure of financial risk, yet many fundamental problems remain unsolved. The main difficulty is that the probability distribution of the financial returns is unknown. While the empirical cdf provides the basic nonparametric estimator, asset return data exhibit stylized properties, such as heavy tails, nonlinear dependence, skewness, volatility clustering etc. that are not adequately captured by a basic statistical model. Hence, the most important question arises.

How to estimate the distribution function of the financial returns, and consequently estimate the VaR and the coherent risk measures, when the underlying distribution is unknown?

This question motivates an extensive research in statistical and mathematical finance. There are many approaches available in the literature which includes parametric approach, historical simulation, GARCH-type volatility models, extreme value theory, copula-based models, and recently machine learning and deep learning techniques. Each method attempts to balance the statistical accuracy, computational efficiency, and robustness under changing market conditions.

Another active area of research focuses on constructing new risk measures or coherent risk measures that remain practical for estimation of finite samples. These research problems provide insight that measuring financial risk is an active field where probability theory, statistics, and optimization interact to address the uncertainty that exists in the financial market.
